Four weeks ago most of the machine came off the printers. On 18 October a printed one poured soda through a printed faucet, though its cold carbonated water still comes from a store-bought carbonator. In the same weeks a faucet was approved for production, and the box was redrawn around what the build taught.

## Soda from the printed box

The new prototype is the printed enclosure with the flavor side inside it — the two pumps, the eight flavor valves, the flow meter, the main board and the machine display — and a white printed faucet. Its cold carbonated water comes from a Lillium, the store-bought carbonator the first prototype pours from, so it has no refrigeration or CO2 of its own. Three of the four quadrants were fitting together by 7 October. The fourth, back-top, took three tries on the printer by the 11th, and the third went in with some of its supports still trapped inside.

On 18 October Derek reported it connected and serving soda, on the countertop beside the sink with its pump heads and tubes outside the box. It took workarounds. Several parts did not fit as drawn, among them the power inlet and some blind tube passages; those are changed in the drawings but not yet printed. One valve had to be swapped for a plain elbow to make the first pour, and the cause turned up the same day: a valve's flow arrow had been read backwards. Flavor A's valve was fitted the wrong way round, and a reversed valve passes flow whether or not it is switched, so flavor A poured; flavor B's line carried flavor A's valve, so flavor B never pumped. Derek reported it all resolved, though no flow rate or ratio has been measured since. Making this prototype fit, and closing it up, is the next milestone.

## The faucet, approved

On 13 October Derek called a complete black Industrial faucet the best faucet print so far. With its one flaw fixed — the print's seam had wandered off the back of the gooseneck — it became the faucet planned for production the next day. Later prints lowered its display 4.5 mm, and on 25 October Derek approved the faucet as it stands — the Industrial base, plate and lever, the lowered tip and the Sculpted display cover, at one layer height throughout — with a small gap at one corner of the cover accepted as it is.

![The approved faucet's tip on 25 October, in black PET-GF, with its display under the Sculpted cover.](/update-images/2026-10-29-faucet.png)

White prints taught a lesson about cooling. Two white display covers broke as they were fitted, one from nothing more than pressing the display into it. A cover printed with the part-cooling fan off went on easily — so easily that other parts of that faucet, printed with the fan on, broke while it was being fitted. Glass-filled PET now prints with the fan off, except on the bridges and overhangs that need it.

A printed press tool now seats the soda tube and its seal in the faucet, and on 13 October it made that step far easier than before.

## The pumps go in for good

The slide-out pump cartridge from the last entry is gone, and the sprung tee carrier with it. The pumps now go into the bay once, in the workshop: they enter motor-first from underneath while front-top is still loose, and a broad printed retainer holds their molded skirts against pockets printed into front-top itself, latched by two full-depth snap wings. Four push-to-connect tees, fitted from behind, grip the pump tubes, and the machine display's pump-service page is gone. A machine with a failed pump is replaced, like any other fault, and the returned unit comes back to the workshop. All of this is drawn and not yet printed.

## The box, redrawn

The machine display's cover now prints face up with no supports, held in by flat wings at its sides, and was accepted with a slight bow. The nameplate prints face up too, with raised white lettering.

![The nameplate and the rings that letter the rear fittings, printed in early October and laid on a printed panel.](/update-images/2026-10-29-rings.png)

The box's screws changed twice. On 15 October six small M3 screws gave way to M5s through interlocking printed blocks, in black oxide, because the inside of the machine should look right too. Since the 25th it closes with four fully threaded M5 screws about 4 in long, two up through the handholds and two down through the roof. The handholds' printed covers are gone; their bare printed ceilings are the grip.

Inside, the back of the box was rearranged between 22 and 26 October to leave the funnel room. The main board now lies flat on the cold core's lid, the power supply and relays stand on back-top's west wall with the flow meter on its east wall, and back-top's access panel is gone. The rear wall's six fittings sit two by three: water and CO2 at the top, the vent and soda in the middle, the flavors at the bottom. None of this is printed yet, and every change to the box's shape is on [Changes](/changes).

## The cold core

A level float now prints in one piece in ASA Aero, the print pausing partway for a ring magnet to be dropped in and covered over. The first one worked on 3 October, and one design now serves all three level columns, the carbonator's and both reservoirs'.

The last entry said nothing printed had held water. That was wrong. A clear PETG reservoir printed on 30 May held water for several hours with its fittings and gaskets, and both September reservoirs held too, from a recipe that takes about 19 hours. Since 21 October Mark1 has printed thirteen more at about 5¾ hours each, chasing leaks where the floor meets the wall. On the 26th a left reservoir with more overlap between its walls and infill came off holding water so far; its right-hand twin is untested. None has yet been tested with a sealed lid, cold, or with syrup.

The cold core's shell — the printed box that holds the carbonator, its cooling coil and both reservoirs, with foam poured around them — printed at production finish on 27 and 28 October, 1,450 g in about 31½ hours. Derek accepted it with one small but very noticeable seam, inside the machine, and a shell with a channel for that seam to sit in started printing on the 29th.

The funnel now lifts off for cleaning. Its outlet is a square silicone plug pressed over a short stub of tube held in a printed snap-in cradle, accepted on 2 October, and the funnel grew to 698 mL (23.6 fl oz) in the redrawn box. Its mold became two printed shells around a stainless rod; the first cavity print failed and took a hotend heater with it. No funnel has been cast.

On the gas side, the cylinder regulator is now a Taprite, to be set at 75 PSI, and inside the machine a fixed 44 PSI push-fit regulator and a check valve feed the carbonator; a matching regulator limits the tap water that fills the reservoirs for cleaning. Printed TPU elbows replaced bought ones at the water pump, accepted on 22 October after about seventeen trials and not yet pressure-tested. In the drawings, the backflow preventer's vent now runs its own tube up to an outlet at the faucet, where any discharge shows in the sink. The jet has not been made, and the carbonator's water pump has not run.

## The phone

The phone app now does more than update the boards. Through the same path — Bluetooth to the faucet, then by wire to the main board — it reads the machine's status, sets the flavor, the pour ratio and the sound, and runs Fill, Clean and Prime. Adding a machine uses Apple's own setup sheet, which shows the faucet and lists only machines nearby. Any phone can read a machine, but changing anything takes a trusted phone: both displays ask "Trust this iPhone?", and one tap answers. The app collects nothing. It has run against the bench boards from an iPad.

Cleaning now runs each pump while tap water fills its reservoir, so the tube that holds syrup between pours is rinsed with no extra joint; that is in the published firmware but has not run on a machine.

## The shop

Nothing has been welded yet; the shop's month went into the tools for it. A six-axis gun positioner was designed on 4 October and set aside two days later for something far simpler: two motorized joints on the weld rotator's base, each on a 50:1 harmonic reducer, aiming the hand-held laser welder's gun while a camera learns the aim in dry runs. Its 35-page guide is on [the drawings shelf](/drawings), and none of it is built. A computer now reads the laser welder's controller over an isolated serial link.

The drawings shelf became a searchable library, with Letter-size shop guides, a guide to the water inlet's jet, and nine dimensioned drawings of finished parts, from the carbonator's end cap to the evaporator coil. A camera kit was ordered on 26 October to film the first welds, and the 3D scanner measured the welder's gun, the backflow preventer, both new regulators and several fittings.

## For the buyer

The three printed 24-page install guides arrived on 25 September, the day of the last entry. The guide has since become a 32-page book, 9 × 7 in landscape and perfect-bound, and the separate quick-start sheet is retired; page 3 now draws every item in the kit, with a checklist of what the buyer supplies. Three copies were ordered on 5 October and shipped on the 12th. The guide is on [the drawings shelf](/drawings), and a printed copy is now priced into every machine.

## What one costs

The parts for one machine now come to $1,459.92, up from $1,374.74. Most of the rise is the gas side's new regulators and new push-fit elbows, with the printed install guide now counted in; printed TPU elbows and the printed floats cost less than what they replaced. Building one takes 12 h of hands-on work and 193.9 printer-hours, about 15.4 lb of filament; the cold core's shell, now printed at production finish, accounts for 16 of the new printer-hours.

[Price & Cost](/cost) now opens with the planned sale price, $4,495 a machine, against a build cost of $2,659.59 with the hands-on work priced at $100 an hour, and charts how many machines it takes to earn back what the project has spent.
