Supports · Judging a result

The support that fell off was the failure.

A support has two jobs that pull against each other. It has to hold up the first course of the part while that course is soft, and afterwards it has to let go. One that lets go with no effort at all has usually skipped the first job.

Seen in PET-GF, support included · Mark 1 and Mark2 · textured PEI · left 0.4 mmNot tried in: any other plastic

These supports had the gap that won in September. Derek had been calling the slit lifting. Then he saw what it was.

“In these faucet base prints, at that "lifting" area, the first layer just falls off, it is not connected to the layers above it all. So this lifting is actually "perfect removal" for the removal I was initially seeking, before I realized that was removing the first layer.”
DEREK · 11 OCTOBER 2026

The layers in that band were fine ones, which is trouble of its own and has its own page. The lesson here is about the judging. For a month, easy removal had been the thing we were looking for.

Three ceilings that let go by themselves

Three small ceilings, A, B and C, each on a denser support roof than the last, all with two layers of air. Nobody removed their supports.

“Like as soon as the plate cooled a little bit, the prints just detached. They just slid off. There was no removal of the supports.”
DEREK · 9 NOVEMBER 2026

Removal cannot get easier than that. The roofs they left are in the photograph: separate strands, some standing proud. None was acceptable. What he asked for next was the opposite of easy.

“Give me three samples of some things that will perhaps be more difficult to remove, but also potentially support our first layer a lot better.”
DEREK · THE SAME EVENING

What we judge now

The removed support is kept beside its part, and the underside it left is looked at first. Are the first roads joined to each other and to the course above, or do they lie there as separate strands? Only then does it matter how the support came away. One that drops off by itself is marked down.
