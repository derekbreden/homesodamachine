# work-as-datum on workspace-as-structure (wave 2)

My view is that the corner's best reference is the end plate itself, not the
room, the bench or the rotator. Theirs is that the workspace is the first
positioning stage. These notes work on two of their ideas where that
difference does the most: the **table-opening gantry** (their deepest) and the
**lidded mouth** (their roughest). Short notes on the other three follow, then
what transfers in each direction.

Sketch: `explorers/work-as-datum/sketches/xw-collar-centre-and-lid.svg`
(generated by `make_exchange_sketch.py`). Numbers:
`explorers/work-as-datum/exchange_calcs.py`.

**A correction first.** Their `geom.py` applies the scene's 35° hole-dial
offset (`main.js`: `holeRotation = holeDegrees − 35`); my wave-1
`pose_geometry.py` did not. Their geometry is right: at the opening pose the
nozzle is ~5 mm above the rim, the housing sits ~112 mm off the tube axis
toward −Y, and the column above the axis is clear. My wave-1 claim that the
gun body sits over the axis (quoted in the digest) was wrong. I have
corrected my files. Everything below uses the corrected pose.

## 1. Table-opening gantry — "tube change without touching the gun"

### The difficulty, in the variant they developed

They load from the side by dropping the shelf ~70 mm. Their payoff line is:
"the gun, wire and umbilical never move between tubes, so the pose is
repeated by construction, and Z is repeated by a screw approached from one
direction."

What the crank repeats is the height of the **nest**. The corner sits at
nest + tube length − 6.35. Three things in that chain are not repeated by
construction:

- **Tube length.** OnlineMetals' published cut tolerance is ±3.2 mm. At the
  opening pose a corner 1 mm low puts the dot 1 mm up the wall with focus
  1.4 mm off; 1 mm high puts it 0.64 mm onto the plate
  (`datum_budget.py` §1, corrected). At full tolerance the dot misses by
  2–3 mm.
- **Radial runout.** The corner's radius comes from the rotator axis (via
  shelf, posts and collar) plus the tube's runout on it. The indicator can't
  reach the OD inside the opening. They offer two routes: indicate with the
  shelf dropped, or read the bore lip from above.
- **Face tilt** of the plate relative to the spin axis.

Their break 5 names the first item and proposes a per-tube Z trim while
watching the dot, or a touch-off on the top rim. Either way, every tube needs a
measurement, so the pose is repeated by re-finding it.

### The assumption behind it

The workpiece is a passive passenger of the rotator: locate the nest and you
have located the joint. The rim touch-off relaxes that a little, but it
still references the saw-cut rim. The plate was set 6.35 mm below the rim by a
spacer that bridges the rim's high points, and it is the plate, not the rim,
that forms the fillet.

### Repair W1 — a centre hung from the collar, on the plate's own centre

Put a small tailstock in their structure (sketch, top panel):

- **On the work.** Two 316 hex nipples go finger-tight into the plate's two
  tapped ports. A seat bar drops over them and carries a 90° countersink
  (brass or hardened insert) at the midpoint between the ports. This is the
  seat from my compass. The nipples are hollow, so the first-closure purge
  still vents.
- **On the collar.** An arm reaches from the collar's free +Y edge (their own
  finding: the gun is entirely on −Y) to the tube axis, lying 40–60 mm above
  the plate. It carries a vertical spring plunger: a Ø12 shaft in a flanged
  LM12 bushing, a compression spring, and a 1/2 in hardened ball at the tip.
  A dial on the shaft top reads the plunger's position.
- **Setup per tube.** Crank the shelf up until the ball enters the
  countersink and the dial reads its zero. Lock the four post clamps. Nothing
  else is adjusted.

What that does:

| Error in their chain | With W1 |
|---|---|
| Tube length (±3.2 mm) and recess | Gone: the shelf goes to wherever the plate centre meets the collar's zero |
| Radial runout on the rotator axis | Gone: the ball-in-cone forces the plate centre onto the plunger axis, which is fixed to the collar; the tube pivots in its nest (M3 adjusters backed off to non-contact) |
| Shelf, post and rotator lateral compliance | Out of the radial loop: the loop is gantry → collar → arm → ball → plate |
| Loose tube in the nest | Clamped down by the 30–50 N plunger preload |
| Face tilt | **Remains**: ±(face runout)/2 at the corner. One indicator reading of the plate face per tube, from their collar-mounted magnetic datum |
| Plate-edge vs port-pair eccentricity, slip gap | **Remains**: ~±0.19 mm radial RSS (my estimate), a fixed once-per-rev sinusoid per plate |

Numbers (`exchange_calcs.py`):

- A 20 × 20 mm aluminium arm, 100 mm long, deflects 11–18 µm under 30–50 N,
  and that deflection is constant.
- A 90° cone under 30–50 N gives 30–50 N of centring force. Rim friction at
  the nest (μ 0.3) is 13–21 N, so the tube top follows the ball.
- If the plunger is 0.2 mm off the rotator axis, the tube tilts 4.5 arcmin
  and its bottom rim rocks ~0.08 mm per revolution. Aligning once with the
  indicator makes that negligible.
- **Side loading still works.** The nipples and seat stand 18.6 mm above the
  rim. With the shelf dropped 70 mm under a 30 mm top there is a 40 mm gap,
  leaving 21 mm to spare. The ball simply parts from the countersink as the
  shelf drops; no retract is needed.
- **Second closure.** Invert the vessel and put the seat on the new top
  plate. The same zero applies, and nothing in their procedure changes.
- The camera stays on the +Y side, placed toward +X so its sight line to the
  dot doesn't cross the arm on the Y axis.

**Branch W1b: a sprung shelf against a rigid centre.** Make the plunger a
rigid stop and put the compliance in the shelf instead: soft springs between
the shelf and a rotator deck, guided by pins. The crank then always goes to
one WELD mark, and the springs absorb ±3.2 mm of tube length while holding the
plate centre up against the fixed centre.

- Numbers: rotator plus vessel is ~92–98 N. A total spring rate of ~4.7 N/mm
  with ~30 mm precompression keeps the preload between ~30 and 60 N over the
  whole length range.
- Gain: during the weld, both Z and radius are referenced to the collar,
  not only at setup.
- Cost: vertical guidance and a torque restraint for the rotator deck, and
  the rotator no longer sits on anything rigid.

### What remains

- Face tilt is not followed. The plate-centre datum is not better radially
  than an indicated axis; it wins by making the shelf's crank position and
  the tube's length irrelevant.
- Nipples go in, and the seat goes on and comes off, for every closure.
- W1 needs open, tapped ports at weld time. The procedure provides them.

## 2. Lidded mouth — a seed, grown from the work side

### The difficulty, in the variant as seeded

As seeded, "a stationary lid a few millimetres above the turning tube's rim,
hung from the collar", carries a nose ring locating the gun ~40 mm from the
dot, with the tail on the countertop or a sled. They name heat, the ring as
the precise element, hidden view, and loading.

From my side, the specific problem is that a collar-hung lid cannot sit a few
millimetres above a rim whose height changes by ±3.2 mm from tube to tube and
wanders ±0.15 mm per revolution. Its clearance has to cover the worst tube,
and the nose ring (the precise element, as they say) is referenced to the
room while the corner under it moves.

### The assumption behind it

The lid belongs to the workspace.

### Repair L1 — the lid rides the plate and parks on the collar

The lid becomes my compass frame, grown into a lid (sketch, lower panel):

- **Riding.** A 3 mm stainless or aluminium sheet disc, Ø~150, sits on the
  plate through the same port seat (centre pin) and three all-stainless ball
  transfers on the plate face at r = 40, at 50°, 170° and 280°. Its underside
  is 3 mm above the rim. That gap depends only on the recess and rim
  waviness (~0.1–0.3 mm, estimate), not on tube length or runout, so the lid
  can sit closer to the rim than any workspace lid could.
- **The notch.** At the corrected pose the nozzle tip is at z = 11.4, level
  with the lid. The barrel crosses lid height at r ≈ 51–55, azimuth −9° to
  −17°, and the wire at r ≈ 54–57, azimuth −10° to −22°. The lid needs a
  notch from about −35° to +10°, for r > 42. It still covers ~85% of the
  mouth, and the plume leaves through the notch, where an extraction hood
  belongs.
- **Park and pick-up.** Lugs on the lid reach over the collar and stand 5 mm
  above it while the lid rides a nominal tube.
  - Drop the shelf more than 5 mm: the lid lands on the collar and parks,
    and the plate parts from it.
  - Raise it: the plate's seat and balls pick the lid up again.
  - A tube 3.2 mm short still leaves 1.8 mm under the lugs, so the pick-up
    window covers the whole cut tolerance.
  - A tall pin on a lug, sliding in a slot in the collar, holds azimuth in
    both states.
- **Gun: nose on the work, tail on the workspace (L1a).**
  - A split nose ring on a post from the lid holds the nozzle cone 10 mm
    above its tip, 26 mm from the dot.
  - The gun's tail rests in a cradle on the collar, ~220 mm behind the ring.
    The cradle carries the weight and the trigger reaction.
  - Any motion d of the work reaches the dot as d × 26/220 ≈ 0.12d, and the
    gun tilts d/220: 0.02 mm and 0.04° for runout, 0.38 mm and 0.83° at full
    cut tolerance.
  - The precise element sits beside the corner and is referenced to the
    corner. The coarse element sits 220 mm away on the workspace, and its
    errors are attenuated about eightfold.
- **Whole gun on the lid (L1b).** Full following, but the lid must then
  carry the gun's weight against a plate seat. The housing's centre of mass
  is ~112 mm off the hub, which needs a balancer at the centre of mass or a
  counterweight. That is my compass's unsolved problem, so L1a is the better
  first form.
- **Its other functions become work-referenced too.** A camera on the lid's
  +Y side sees the dot in plate coordinates at fixed geometry. The gas port
  sits at a fixed position relative to the corner. The 3 mm gap is constant,
  which is better for shielding and containment than a clearance sized for
  the worst tube.

### What remains

- Heat and spatter on a lid 3 mm above the rim, 10–20 mm from the puddle at
  the notch edge. Metal sheet, a replaceable notch liner, and the camera
  window behind a slot rather than facing the plume.
- The nose ring grips the copper nozzle cone. The ring must not be the
  interlock's contact path, and its effect on nozzle heat is unknown.
- L1a's residual (0.12d) means a per-tube Z trim is still worth doing for
  long tubes. **Combined with W1**, tube length is already absorbed, so the
  lid only follows face tilt and runout, and L1a's residual drops to the
  0.02 mm level.
- Top loading (their retract-along-−Y variant): the gun slides back out of
  an openable nose ring and the lid lifts off with a handle. Side loading
  needs nothing: the lid parks by itself.

## 3. Shorter notes on the other three

- **Countertop sled.** Their 3-2-1 sled (three feet on a rim-height plane,
  fence, stop, spring) references a workspace plane.
  - Put that plane on the work: the sled's feet on the L1 lid's top face,
    with the fence and stop on the lid. The sled keeps everything they built
    (lift off and return, six stationary actuators for the automated vision),
    and its reference then follows the plate.
  - What stops me recommending it outright: the lid then carries the sled's
    weight on the plate seat, the same moment problem as my compass. With W1
    in place instead, their plane stays on the collar, and the corner is
    already fixed relative to it.
- **Fixed gun, moving shelf.** Their live runout following, where a stepper
  moves the work in X from the console's degrees or a camera, is active and
  corrects what the camera sees, including the plate-edge residual my passive
  centre can't remove. W1's centre is passive and removes the bulk (runout,
  length) with no electronics. Together: W1 for the bulk, their slow X
  correction for the last ±0.1–0.2 mm. The correction then belongs on the
  plunger arm or the gun's X, not on the rotator, which is forced by the
  centre.
- **Drop-in collar.** Their one-plate collar is exactly the structure my
  between-centres idea lacked. I had a mast on a sub-plate; their collar is a
  sub-plate at rim height, with the gantry and the tailstock arm both on it,
  and all loops short. Their magnetic datum beside the mouth is best pointed
  at the plate face near the corner, the one thing W1 doesn't fix.

## What their ideas do that my arrangements lack

- **The gun and its cables never move between tubes** (side loading by
  shelf drop). My compass lifts the gun on a balancer for every tube.
- **A rim-height structure right beside the joint.** It gives a place to park
  things (the lid's catch), a cable anchor at the right height, a weight path
  for the gun's tail that isn't the plate, and a free +Y side for arms and
  cameras. The weight path is what my compass's worst problem needed: the
  gun's weight and trigger reaction go to the workspace while the nose
  follows the work.
- **The countertop mouth:** snip from above, see into the recess, purge hose
  straight up through an open shelf.
- **Separating weight from positioning** (sled: the plane carries, light
  pushers locate). My compass collapses weight onto the balancer and
  position onto the plate. Their pattern suggests the gun's weight should go
  to the workspace and only position should come from the work.

## What transfers

- **To theirs:**
  - W1's collar centre makes "repeated by construction" true at the joint.
  - L1 makes the lidded mouth a work-referenced lid that parks itself.
  - The plate face, not the rim, is the right Z touch-off.
- **To mine:**
  - Between-centres is rebuilt on their collar (tailstock arm from the +Y
    edge, gun on the −Y side).
  - The compass frame gets a park/catch on the collar.
  - The compass gun goes nose-on-work, tail-on-workspace. This is my answer
    to the force-quiet problem they will raise, and I'll develop it in wave 3.
- **Shared finding:** once the plate centre is forced to a workspace datum,
  every workspace element near the mouth (gantry, sled plane, lid, camera)
  sees a corner that doesn't move from tube to tube. The work-referenced
  element is small; the workspace does everything else.
