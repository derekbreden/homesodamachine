# a10 Dock, tack, cut: then each contact crimped alone in a keyed steel nest

## Picture it

A combination. From this directory, [a2](a2-two-pallets-meet.md): a fanned
ribbon docked onto a carrier segment, the carrier as fixture, continuity before
force. From force-and-form:
[f9](../../force-and-form/ideas/f9-tack-station-feeds-crimp-station.md)'s light
gang tack handing contacts, already on their wires, to a heavy single-contact
station; [f3](../../force-and-form/ideas/f3-knee-micropress.md)'s knee,
re-touch and neck blade; [f6](../../force-and-form/ideas/f6-two-blades-two-drives.md)'s
separate insulation blade; and the steel C of
[f4](../../force-and-form/ideas/f4-crimp-head-goes-to-the-wire.md) and
[f8](../../force-and-form/ideas/f8-narrow-press-at-the-housing-mouth.md) with its
throat toward the wire. The tack itself is change-the-question's
[c1b](../../change-the-question/ideas/c1b-tack-first.md). Sketch:
[`../sketches/a10-dock-tack-then-nest.svg`](../sketches/a10-dock-tack-then-nest.svg)
(schematic).

The pairing: a2 places every contact of a ribbon end in one motion, from strip,
in orientation, checked before force, but keeps each contact on a 0.8 × 0.2 mm
tab with 0.10–0.14 mm of elastic lift through the heavy stroke [calc X §5]. f9
gives the heavy station a contact already fixed to its wire, but its tack lays
half-rows into loose contacts and splits the ribbon into two planes. Here the
tack is made **on the strip, right after docking,** while the carrier is still
clamped. The carrier's job ends at the tack; every contact then belongs to the
ribbon, one strip pitch (~7.1 mm) from its neighbours, so the heavy station's
jaws get ~11 mm of room [w3 §3] with no planes, fork, fold or threading.

**Where things start.**
- **The ribbon pallet** (an a1-type pallet whose lid face is the split root)
  arrives parted ([a7](a7-zip-station.md)), fanned to strip pitch, flush-cut and
  ring-stripped at the fan block's face ([a8](a8-rolling-ring-scorer.md)), every
  tip touched off. Each ribbon of a pair on its own.
- **The strip pallet** on the bench is a steel plate with slot pins for the
  carrier and **a hardened rail under the row of insulation barrels only**, its
  top flush with the contacts' floor and its rear edge a shear edge at the
  contacts' rear. Everything forward of the rail overhangs, so each lance hangs
  free; on a flat shelf each contact would sit propped 7.6–15.9° [w3 §5].
- **The strip segment.** The person snips N + 2 contacts off the strip, removing
  the position under a trimmed conductor for J2 and J7, and lays the segment on
  the slot pins; a clamp bar holds the carrier between conductor paths.
- **The tack-and-shear block** beside it: a notched steel **tack comb**, one
  insulation-barrel notch per contact at the measured strip pitch, driven by a
  lever to a hard stop; and a floating shear under the carrier. POWERTEC 305CM
  push-pull toggle clamps (two for $18.25, 500 lb hold [Prime]) can serve as the
  levers.
- **The heavy station,** elsewhere on the bench: a fist-sized steel C standing
  fixed, spine ahead of the boxes, throat toward the wire side.
  - **Upper jaw:** f3's knee drives the conductor crimper to a geometric bottom;
    f6's insulation blade slides beside it on its own small drive and wedge; a
    0.001 mm indicator reads across the dies (Clockwise DITR-0105, $52.99
    [Prime]).
  - **Lower jaw:** a narrow post carrying a **keyed nest**: a box slot with
    lead-in chamfers, a lance relief, a front stop for the box, the conductor and
    insulation anvils, a button load cell under the anvils.
  - **In front of the throat:** a ribbon-pallet seat on an XYZ stage.

**What moves.**
1. **Dock.** The person sets the ribbon pallet on three balls on hardened seats;
   magnets pull it home. Every conductor goes into every open contact (pressed
   in by a finger comb if the wings are narrower than the jacket [calc F §4]).
   The far-end port reads each conductor to the grounded carrier; the camera
   photographs the row.
2. **Tack.** The lever drives the tack comb to its stop. Every insulation barrel
   of the end closes loosely on its jacket in one stroke: 99–396 N for a 3P,
   165–660 N for a 5P [w3 §3, from c1b's per-contact range]. The conductor
   barrels stay open.
3. **Cut.** The tack comb stays down as the pad; the shear drives the carrier
   down past the rail's edge and cuts every tab: 150–480 N for a 3P, 250–800 N
   for a 5P [calc X §5]. Tab length is the rail edge's position against the slot
   pins.
4. **Lift.** The ribbon pallet lifts off. Every contact hangs from its own
   conductor by the tack, all with the same roll. Carrier and spares stay on the
   pins as scrap.
5. **Seat** the pallet on the heavy station's three balls.
6. **Into the nest, one contact at a time.** With the crimper raised 5 mm, the
   stage carries contact k box-first ~1.2 mm above the anvils (the lance, 0.6–0.9
   mm below the floor, clears them), then lowers it: the box enters its slot on
   the chamfers, the lance its relief. The stage then moves the contact +Y until
   the box meets the front stop, tenths of a newton through ~9 mm of overhang
   that buckles only at ~1.8 N [w3 §3]. The neighbours hang one strip pitch away
   on each side, beside the post and outside the crimper.
7. **Identity and look.** The far-end port reads which conductor touches the
   grounded nest; only conductor k may read closed. Both blades come down to
   capture height, and the camera checks strands in the U, none over a wing tip,
   the insulation edge in the window.
8. **Crimp the conductor barrel.** The knee goes to straight; force is logged
   against true die gap; then a re-touch at ~10 N reads crimp height (f3).
9. **Re-form the insulation barrel.** f6's blade descends alone from the tack's
   height to the height f6's sweep found for this silicone, watching for the
   much steeper slope of wing tips reaching copper.
10. **Pull.** A 0.3 mm blade drops into the neck in front of the conductor crimp
    and bears on the box's rear face; a small jaw at the throat grips the jacket
    3–5 mm behind the insulation barrel and pulls ~20 N. The stage carries none
    of it.
11. **Out.** The crimper rises; an ejector pin lifts the box out of its slot; the
    stage raises the contact 1.2 mm, backs out, and indexes one strip pitch.

**What locates what, and the reference for fixed.** **"Fixed" is the slot pins
until the tack, and the nest's box stop after it.**
- At docking: carrier on slot pins (±0.05 mm stamping [assumption]); the seat to
  the same plate (±0.01–0.02 mm); the fan block ±0.1 mm; capture as a2; axially,
  the strip line against the slot pins (±0.2 mm stacked).
- **The tack freezes** that axial position and each contact's roll onto its
  jacket.
- At the heavy station the nest locates the contact to ±0.02–0.05 mm
  [force-and-form placement_budget]; the stage only has to deliver the box into
  the chamfers' ±0.3–0.5 mm. Bending the free conductor 0.3–0.5 mm sideways
  takes 1–3 mN, and squaring a 5–10° roll 0.03–0.2 N·mm, against a tack that
  holds 0.4–9 N and 0.4–2.5 N·mm [w3 §3; force-and-form calc wave2 §5]. With
  10 mm or more of free conductor, stage error never reaches the tack.
- **Which way the tack is loaded:** the box goes into its slot vertically, so
  entry friction loads the tack across the jacket, where the wrapped wings hold
  it. A box-first push into a slot (c1b's) would load it along the jacket, where
  a loose tack may hold as little as 0.1–0.4 N.

**What drives the crimp and carries its force.** The knee inside the C, pushed
at 60–160 N by a NEMA 17 lead screw [force-and-form drives §B] (the Iverntech
42HD6039-05 integrated Tr8×2 motor, $27.99 [Prime, thin listing]). The force
loop is C → knee → crimper → crimp → anvils → load cell → C. The ribbon pallet
and stage carry none of it. The tack block's 0.1–0.7 kN (tack) and 0.15–0.8 kN
(shear) close through its own steel.

**How it knows.** Before any force: conductor-to-carrier continuity and the
docked row's frame. At the heavy station: identity through the far-end port
(a stage mis-index, or a J4/J7 recipe error made at loading, stops here); f3's
capture look, force-against-gap curve and re-touch height; f6's insulation
curve; the pull.

**What the person does.** Per ribbon end, about 55 s: snips and lays a strip
segment (~20 s), sets the pallet (~10 s), pulls the tack and shear levers
(~15 s), moves the pallet to the heavy station (~10 s) [w3 §3, estimate]. About
13 minutes a unit plus loading the pallets and insertion. The heavy station runs
53–106 min a unit unattended at 1–2 min per crimp; it calls once per ribbon end,
14 times a unit, fewer with a pallet rack at the station [estimate].

## Steps it covers and what it hands back

- **Machine and fixture:** placement of every contact by one docking, the tack,
  the tab cut, each crimp in a keyed nest with height and insulation re-form,
  identity, pull; with a7, a8 and a6, also split, strip and insert.
- **Person:** strip segments, docking and two levers per end, moving pallets,
  far ends.
- **Contacts:** 81 a unit at N + 2 with trimmed positions snipped out: $1.90 from
  the SXH reel, $3.82 from 100-piece strip, $0.64 from the CJT clone reel
  [w3 §3; calc F §7].

## Numbers

- **Tack comb.** Each notch mouth is as wide as the open wings (up to ~3.25 mm),
  leaving 3.55–3.85 mm of steel between mouths at 6.8–7.1 mm pitch, so one pass
  tacks the whole end (c1b at 3.4 mm needs two). A loose tack's profile tolerance
  is a tenth or two, so laser-cut steel at ±0.127 mm serves (SendCutSend,
  2–4 days [force-and-form f7]). The plate is about as thick as the insulation
  barrel is long, 1.2–1.5 mm, so it stays out of the window.
- **Parted length:** 21 / 25 / 30 mm (3P / 4P / 5P) fanned alone to ~7 mm
  [calc §2]. Cut after the fan (order A of [a5](a5-part-fan-strip-in-the-pallet.md)),
  the outer conductors carry 1.2–2.5 mm into the finished loom, a 3–5 mm arc
  [calc F §5]; stripped before the split with an equal-path fan (order B), none.

## Branches and variants kept beside it

- **[a10b](a10b-tacked-row-into-the-hand-tool.md), by hand:** the same dock, tack
  and shear on levers, then each tacked contact crimped in today's SN-2549. No
  motor.
- **With force-and-form's cartridge:** the heavy station is f7's cartridge with a
  keyed nest in the VEVOR AP-1 arbor press ($61.90, 150 mm opening, 90 mm plate
  [Prime]); the person strokes it to the cartridge's own stop.
- **Every k-th conductor** (force-and-form's f5-k with a2): the split falls to
  what a 3–4 mm lift needs, 12–20 mm, at the cost of k docks and tacks per end;
  at the heavy station the neighbours are then 1.7 mm away and must be lifted,
  which gives back the 11 mm jaw allowance.
- **On loose kit contacts:** [a2c](a2c-loose-contact-cassette.md)'s keyed nest
  bar at 5 mm replaces the strip; the pocket's rear shoulder reacts the tack; a
  5P splits 23 mm; pocket loading returns.
- **With a curl tack** (force-and-form's
  [f3b](../../force-and-form/ideas/f3b-two-station-forming.md) station A as the
  gang tack at strip pitch): 180–580 N per contact, 0.9–2.9 kN for a 5P, so the
  tack block needs a steel local stop; curled wings trap the strands, not only the
  jacket.

## What it contributes

- **Placement from strip, and a heavy station that only receives:** the carrier
  places and orients, the tack hands over, and the heavy station holds one
  contact in a keyed steel nest with room around it.
- **Identity before force** at a single-contact station, through the far end.

## Major unresolved problems

- **The tack's grip on silicone,** and the tack-first crimp (force-and-form f3b's
  sectioning experiment; the hand test in [a10b](a10b-tacked-row-into-the-hand-tool.md)).
- **Whether the loosely closed insulation wings hold each contact on the rail
  edge through 50–160 N of tab shear.** The comb's stop keeps it from crushing
  them further; the rail edge's position decides the rest.
- **The nest's conductor anvil under the lance:** it needs the neck t ≥
  0.34–0.74 mm, or a lance slot in the anvil [w3 §5]; the same t decides the
  0.3 mm pull blade.
- **Lifting a box out of a slot** with 0.05 mm clearance, by ejector.
- **The strip pitch** (the $4.71 strip), and **21–30 mm of split** behind the
  housing (Derek's call).
