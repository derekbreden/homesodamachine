# Notebook: borrowed (Borrowed from elsewhere)

Framing: mass-produced products sold for other purposes already do parts of this job. For each piece of the job I ask which product does it, what I would take from it, what has to change, and how fast it is in hand. Tags on numbers: [Derek] [repo] [manual] [derived] [unknown], plus **illustrative** for numbers I invented to make a picture.

## Wave 1, step 2: the wide list (before developing anything)

Written first, one line each, silly ones included. B-numbers are arrangement numbers, not ids of ideas that were developed; developed ideas get `borrowed-NN-slug`.

1. **B1 Pano-head pivot.** A photographer's panoramic head rotates a camera about a settable no-parallax point (yaw + pitch on a nodal slide). Set the point to the dot. Immediate flaw: a shaft along an axis through the dot pierces the tube wall or the endcap.
2. **B2 Dobsonian rings.** A telescope's pivots are big arcs and rings that turn about a point with no shaft on it. Printed ball races (Derek already prints the rotator's 165 mm race) as rings around the three virtual axes: yaw ring above the rim around the barrel, a large pitch ring outside the wall, a roll collar round the grip.
3. **B3 Gimbal on a gantry.** A hobby or handheld three-axis brushless gimbal, balanced on the gun so its axes meet at the centre of mass; a printer/CNC gantry moves the whole gimbal to cancel the dot's swing. Orientation from one product, translation from another, G-code and a serial gimbal protocol in between.
4. **B4 Encoded balanced arm.** A gas-spring monitor arm or Anglepoise-type arm carries the weight; a magnetic encoder (AS5600-class) on every joint turns it into a passive measuring arm. The operator still moves the gun; software moves nothing and observes the pose. Ladder up to brakes and small trim motors.
5. **B5 Machinist DRO stack with an AI spotter.** Cross-slide vise, lab jack and geared photo head, each with a cheap digital-readout scale; the AI reads scales and a camera and tells a person which knob to turn and by how much (the coupling between orientation and dot position is done by software, the hands do the moving).
6. **B6 Guide-star calibration.** Astrophotography autoguiding: nudge each axis, watch the "star" (the dot) in a small camera, solve the pixel-to-axis matrix and the backlash, then guide. An AI can bring up an unknown mechanism without being told its kinematics.
7. **B7 Swing-offset steering.** The gun already has a motor that swings the beam (2 mm swing in the recorded settings) and a screen that shifts the red light left/right. If software can set that offset over RS232, the gun carries its own fine axis across the seam.
8. **B8 Tonearm follower.** Phonograph logic: a counterbalanced two-axis pivot with a ball-transfer foot riding the tube's rim so the rim sets the height and follows the runout; an angle encoder on the arm reads the runout for free. Silly version: the gun is 1 kg, a tonearm carries grams. Useful version: the arm carries only the follower and its own counterweight; the gun is carried elsewhere and is pressed against the follower by a spring.
9. **B9 Actuators from other trades.** Stage-light moving-head yoke (DMX, 16-bit pan/tilt, homing), car power-window regulator or wiper motor (a $15 high-torque geared motor, Hall feedback in the regulator), power-seat rails, standing-desk column for height. Take the axis, not the product.
10. **B10 Balancer plus festoon.** A spring tool balancer or constant-force spring carries the weight from above; the umbilical rides in a curtain-track festoon or a CNC energy chain (torsion-free, guaranteed minimum bend radius). Software has nothing to move here; it is the carrying and the cable.
11. **B11 Leader-follower hand controller.** A small printed leader arm with servo encoders (the LeRobot SO-100/SO-101 pattern) is the hand controller for a large follower carrying the gun; hand demonstrations become recorded episodes an AI can replay or learn from. Silly: the $110 arm cannot carry the gun. Useful: it is the leader, not the follower.
12. **B12 Gun-free dot.** A $15 servo pan/tilt cat-toy laser pointer stands in for the gun's dot so the whole seeing-and-calibrating chain can be run dry, all night, with no gun, no tube runout, no interlock. Also: a SpaceMouse or CNC handwheel as the setup jog input, and one Arca-Swiss dovetail on the shell so the same shell drops into a head, arm, gimbal or gantry.

Not in the list because it is the obvious first one: a used industrial six-axis arm. It stays as the reference the others are measured against, not something I develop.

## Observations that came out of the reading (not ideas yet)

- **The dot is at a corner made of metal.** The dot sits where the 1.65 mm wall meets the plate's top face; every axis line through it starts in solid metal on one side. Donor mechanisms are designed with free air around their pivot, so the first question for each is where its shafts and rings can physically be. [derived from `WK.DIM`, shared-context geometry]
- **The gun already has a fine axis.** Manual p. 25 and pp. 38-39: a "red light alignment" screen moves the red light left or right of the nozzle centre; the laser head contains a motor (p. 17 item 5, p. 20). Sweep direction in the kit's gun is local X, which is across the seam when the barrel is tangent. Whether an offset can be commanded, and over what range, is [unknown]; the RS232 port (p. 16) is documented only as "PC-based supervisory software". [manual]
- **Rotation about the grip-axis line only twists the fibre if the fibre leaves along it.** The reference scene's umbilical stub leaves along the grip axis, so that roll turns the cable about its own axis. Manual p. 20: twisting "strictly forbidden". How much twist per metre matters is [unknown]. [manual, repo scene]
- **Setup versus operating.** The tube length and plate seat depth vary per tube [unknown]; runout is 0.25 / 0.30 mm TIR [repo]. Setup travel (millimetres to centimetres, once per tube) and operating travel (tenths of a millimetre, continuous) are different jobs and donors fit one or the other.

## Wave 1, steps 3 to 6: what was developed and what happened

Chosen for depth because each has geometry or numbers that could change my mind: the ring-pivot family (with the axis map), the gimbal-on-gantry, the encoded arm. Developed to a scene and idea file because each is a different mechanism or method: guide star, swing offset, tonearm. Rough scene for the manual stack with a spotter (a person turns the knobs). Written up as sketches: hand controller, gun-free dot, balancer plus festoon, other trades.

Order of work and what each round showed (numbers are from the scripts in `calc/` or the scene's own tests and are illustrative unless tagged):

1. **Free directions (`calc/free_directions.py`, scene 04).** I first believed no axis line through the dot can hold a bearing. Wrong: a ring bearing needs nothing on its axis, only a free circle. Shafts: at 5 mm radius 38 % of directions fit one-sided and 12 % two-sided; at 10 mm, 1.6 % and 0 %. Rings at 80 mm and R 60: 99 % of directions. That turned the pano-head idea into the ring-pivot idea.
2. **Ring pivots (scene 01).** The roll axis (dot to cable exit) does not run along the grip; it runs in air in front of it and meets the grip only at its lower end, so a roll ring around the grip has to sit at the grip base (my first placement, 228 mm, failed). Frame arm at ring height: yaw limited to +-37 degrees. Overhead arm and strut: +-89 at default sizes; a bigger trunnion ring or yaw bore breaks it again (yaw +-25 with bore 100). Hole 18 to 94 and roll 1 to 71 at bore 62. The ranges trade off; whack-a-mole, not a solution. Added path continuity: a slider jump across a blocked band is not a motion.
3. **Gimbal on gantry (`calc/gimbal_geometry.py`, scene 02).** The dot is C - t g(v, h): roll does not move the dot. Travel for +-30 degrees of yaw and hole at t = 200: 193 x 122 x 173 mm; at t = 120: 116 x 73 x 104. Dot swing without compensation: 3.0 mm per degree of yaw, 3.5 per degree of hole. Angle error 0.05 degrees costs 0.17 mm. The Genmitsu 3018 found on Prime has 40 mm of Z, so a stock hobby CNC is not the frame. Power-off sag is a real design axis (direct-drive vs worm).
4. **Encoded arm (`calc/encoder_error.py`, scene 03).** The Python port reproduces the scene. 12 bit alone: 0.31 mm; 8 bit: 7.5 mm; 0.2 degree INL: 3.1 mm; 0.3 degree offsets: 3.3 mm, 0.6 mm after touch-off. The AS5600 datasheet says 12 bit and +-1 degree system INL maximum: about 15 mm here. Lever arms of the two big joints are about 490 mm (0.85 mm per 0.1 degree each). Conclusion changed my mind: cheap magnetic encoders make a coarse pose recorder, not a fine aligner. The pose recorder is still valuable: it is the only way in this study to obtain the useful travel from a skilled hand. The best-selling gas-spring arm is rated from 4.4 lb; the gun may be lighter.
5. **Guide star (`calc/guide_star_sim.py`, scene 05).** The Python twin reproduces the scene. A naive loop fails beyond about 45 degrees of camera roll (0.04 mm at 45, 0.55 mm at 65, runaway at 75); calibrated it sits at 0.03 / 0.02 mm at any roll. Backlash compensation matters only when the slack is large (12 steps x 0.05 mm). I had written that a guide without it limit-cycles; the simulation did not show that at the default sizes, so the text says so.
6. **Swing offset (scene 06).** At the opening pose 1 mm of centre offset moves the spot 1.83 mm along the corner path; a nulling loop tracks a 1.2 mm / 0.6 mm drift to 0.00. Everything rests on an unknown: can the offset be commanded, and over what range.
7. **Tonearm (scene 07).** Follower removes the face-runout part of the vertical error (1.43 mm to -0.005 mm at 20x exaggeration) but not the radial part or the plate-depth error; force is about 2 N at the default counterweight, 6 N per kg of counterweight at 150 mm.
8. **Spotter (`calc/spotter_rounds.py`).** With a person turning to numbers on a 0.01 mm scale the hand is not the limit: the camera's noise and bias are. One round with a clean camera, about 4 with 0.15 mm noise, and a 0.15 mm bias keeps the true error near 0.15 mm whatever the hand does.

### What broke, what I learned (rounds; details in the exchange and the idea files)

1. **freedom-02's arm forgives 0.09 kg.** The window plot is drawn from friction over (g x reach), which in kilograms is +-0.091 kg at the drawn numbers (0.4 N.m, 0.45 m). The parts are the payload: with a vernier the total is 2.3 kg, the floor is met without ballast, and the heavier payload narrows a gas spring's window from -19..32 to -10..21 degrees. The vernier's own travel (1.2 kg moved 10 mm) uses 29 % of the friction band. The window is where the spring type shows: zero-length spring -20..50, plain coil -20..29, gas -19..32 for a matched 1.5 kg.
2. **freedom-03 is taut because the fibre pulls.** At 0 N of pull the panel reads "all taut" with two lines at 0.1 and 0.2 N. The layout was searched for three pulls; over the sphere of pull directions the worst slackens a line at 0.13 N and 58 % of directions slacken one below 1 N. A robustness search over sixteen million tries with the same lugs and clearance gives 2.6 N with gravity alone, 4.75 N with a layout made for a 10 N downward preload; the same preload on the shipped layout slackens two lines. The sphere is the harsh reading: within +-15 degrees of the scene's own pull direction the shipped layout holds 4.2 N (the searched layout 3.0 N), within +-30 degrees 1.6 N (2.7 N): the search buys robustness to an unknown direction, not margin at a known one.
3. **freedom-08 rings and the fix is a keel.** K 0.42 N.m/rad gives a 1 s period at zeta 0.03 and a first swing twice the static lean; the trim mass has 0.029 N.m (0.2 N of pull at 145 mm). A keel of 0.6 kg on 200 mm takes K to 1.60, makes the trim 3.8 times finer and adds the mass that fills a monitor arm's payload floor. Getting 0.15 mm per newton from gravity needs the cable's line within 0.3 mm of the pivot: it has to be anchored there.
4. **freedom-15 is missing a column.** A fibre clamped at the exit applies a couple as well as a force (up to EI/R, 0.14 N.m for the placeholder), and 0.05 N.m of couple alone moves the dot 6.3 mm with the force anchored at the pivot. A slack service loop is a spring EI/l_loop of the same order as the keel's.
5. **The hexapod's stiffness sits at the dot.** Ring 18 mm above the dot, six legs of 20 N/mm: 0.097 / 0.096 / 0.017 mm/N for a push at the dot, growing about 5x with the ring at 140 mm; the leg stroke for 10 degrees about the dot is 5.5 mm at 18 mm and 19 mm at 140 mm; the gun's body clears the leg cage only within about 8 degrees of clocking.
6. **A script mistake worth keeping:** my first loop over scene settings passed `$cfg` unquoted in zsh, which does not word-split, so every configuration ran as the defaults and the output looked plausible. I found it when a run with `k=50` printed the compliance of `k=20`. Reading a result against the setting that should have changed it is the check.
7. **A model bug I introduced and fixed:** the plain-spring model gave 27 N.m of error at -20 degrees for a free length longer than the shortest spring length (a compressed spring); the slider is limited to 90 mm and the default is 40 mm.

### Kit issues

- `WK.prim.ring(radius, tube, ...)` bakes the radius into the geometry: scaling the mesh scales the tube too (a ring whose radius changes has to be rebuilt; `borrowed-13` does it with `app.scene.remove`).
- `gun.local(...)` and `gun.dir(...)` accept THREE.Vector3 arguments, not only names, and `gun.local(WK.GRIP_BASE_LOCAL.clone())` works; the README lists "nameOrPoint" and does not say a Vector3 is a point. Worth one line.
- `app.ui.set(id, v)` on a radio or slider runs its `onChange`, which is what a preset radio needs; the README lists `set` under "silent" only. Worth one line.
- Custom stages: a grid that uses `repeat(auto-fit, minmax(400px, 1fr))` (not a viewport media query) is the layout that works at both 1280 px and 390 px, because the stage is narrower than the viewport by the controls column.
- The 20 s navigation timeout of `check-scene` failed for freedom in wave 2 under a load average of 50 to 90; mine passed at 1.5 to 2.8 s ready each, but the machine's load was 9 to 32 when I ran them.
